About Pro Gravel Calculator

Pro Gravel Calculator is an independent, free estimating tool for gravel, sand and stone. It turns three measurements — area, depth and material — into the volume, weight and bag count you need before ordering.

Last updated: November 1, 2026

What the calculator does

The calculator supports rectangular, circular and L-shaped areas in imperial or metric units. It reports volume in cubic yards, estimated weight in short tons and the number of 50 lb bags. Every result is an estimate for planning; it is not a substitute for a site survey or a supplier's own quantity calculation.

The site is not a quarry, contractor or materials marketplace. We do not sell aggregate and we do not earn a commission from the suppliers you contact.

How the estimate is calculated

  1. Convert every dimension to feet. Feet and inches are added together as feet + inches ÷ 12. Metres and centimetres are converted to feet.
  2. Calculate the area. Rectangle: length × width. Circle: π × radius². L-shape: Section A + Section B.
  3. Convert depth to feet. Inches are divided by 12.
  4. Calculate volume. cubic yards = area (ft²) × depth (ft) ÷ 27.
  5. Estimate weight. short tons = cubic yards × 27 × density (lb/ft³) ÷ 2,000.
  6. Estimate bags. Total pounds ÷ 50, rounded up to the next whole bag.

Why you may see 2,835 lb/yd³ for crushed stone

The 2,835 lb/yd³ figure is a legitimate reference point: it is 105 lb/ft³, a density often quoted for compacted or higher-specific-gravity crushed stone. This calculator deliberately uses 100 lb/ft³ (2,700 lb/yd³) for crushed stone because that is a more typical loose bulk density for the material most suppliers deliver.

The difference is about 5%. For a 10-ton order that is roughly half a ton, which matters at the scale of a driveway. If your supplier publishes a test density or a weight per cubic yard, use their number instead of our default. The calculator is a planning aid, not a laboratory result for your specific pile.

Allowances, rounding and limits

  • No automatic waste allowance. The result is the geometric volume. Order about 10% extra for compaction, spillage and uneven ground. Increase to 15% for loose rounded stone, sloped sites or a soft subgrade.
  • Two-decimal display. Volume and weight are rounded for readability. Bags are rounded up so you do not see a fractional bag.
  • Compaction changes volume. Gravel is usually sold loose but compacted in place. A 4-inch loose layer can finish thinner after compaction; plan lifts and depths accordingly.
  • Moisture changes weight. Wet sand and gravel weigh more than dry material. Suppliers price by weight or volume; confirm which unit their quote uses.
  • Shape assumptions. The L-shape mode adds two rectangles. It does not account for curves, cut-outs, retaining walls or over-excavation.

Sources and references

Density values are typical planning figures compiled from published aggregate supplier data and standard estimating references. They are presented as ranges in practice, not as universal constants. Our cost guides use the published U.S. price data described on those pages, including:

  • GoodGravel, Gravel Prices by State (October 2026 edition) — median and middle-half prices from 10,101 published listings across 522 U.S. supply yards.
  • U.S. Geological Survey, Crushed Stone Statistics and Information — background on crushed stone production and use.

Where a figure is an illustrative planning example rather than a published measurement, the page says so. We do not present invented customer bills, review counts or search data as facts.

Corrections and editorial standards

If you find an error, a broken source or a density that does not match your supplier's data, please contact us with the page and the evidence. Corrections are reviewed against the source before publication. Commercial relationships do not determine which materials or methods we recommend.

The calculator and guides are written for a U.S. audience, with metric inputs and outputs for international visitors. Local codes, quarry specifications and site conditions always take precedence over a general guide.
